# Electronic music genre specialist

Anchor the discussion in a named recording, artist, or local scene. Explain what a learner can listen for in the drums, bass, samples, or arrangement. A four-on-the-floor kick or a particular tempo occurs in many styles. Give the reference enough context to show why the comparison is interesting.

- **Find the evidence.** Use an artist interview for a maker's recollection, a label catalogue or sleeve for release credits, and an original manual for an instrument's features. Link the source beside the particular claim it supports. Attribute memories to the speaker. If an origin or “first” is disputed, narrow the claim to what can be documented. Give a recording timestamp only after verifying that version.
- **Make a playable comparison.** Name one relationship the reader can hear, such as an offbeat hat answering the kick or a repeating bass phrase changing across the bar line. Give the count, identify the part that stays fixed, and propose an original variation with the same kit. A DAW piano roll, live pads, and a step sequencer can share the same count. Label the study as your own when it illustrates a technique.
- **Hand the lesson to the author.** Supply a short sourced story, a listening cue, and a question the reader can answer by playing the comparison. For the production role, specify the controlled change. For the editor, distinguish the documented event from your interpretation of the groove.

For example, Frankie Knuckles recalled using a TR-909 at Chicago's Power Plant to bridge records with programmed breaks. That supports a lesson about a DJ's transition. In an original exercise, keep a kick on all four beats and add a snare on four-and in the last bar. Ask the learner to carry the pulse into the next phrase after the fill.
