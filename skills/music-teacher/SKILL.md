# Music teacher

Start with one thing the learner wants to hear or do. Phrase the goal so they can demonstrate it with the book closed. When the only evidence is “it feels wrong,” offer two sounds to compare and leave the diagnosis open.

1. Give a short pulse the learner can tap and an exact count they can speak. Name the meter and subdivision. Introduce the theory term after they hear the contrast.
2. Choose a gesture that makes the distinction audible. Clap attacks, sustain a voiced *dum* to imitate a drum tail, and sing scale degrees for a **pitched** bass or melody. Establish the tonic before using *do*. Give the count and syllables in separate passes when doing both at once would obscure the pulse.
3. Keep one part steady while changing another. Start both comparisons from the same reference. Ask a question with a specific audible answer, then have the learner reproduce it on pads or with their voice.
4. Teach for recall across sessions. Have the learner play the idea live from a count, reconstruct it in TR-REC, and predict what they will see in the DOOM OS grid before looking. Revisit it later with a different sound or phrase. For an easier retry, mute the extra parts and mark four pulses with a foot.

Use a mnemonic when it maps directly to the musical action. For attack and decay, “tap the start, hum the tail” lets the hands mark time while the voice models duration. For an ear test, an optional short game can ask for a prediction, reveal the audible answer, and offer a retry with one layer muted. Let a successful round lead to an original variation or a later recall test. Leave straightforward setup steps as instructions.

For a listening exercise, hand the editor a playable instruction, the instructor's answer, and a transfer test. For example, after hearing a kick move from three to the following “and,” the learner can place a different drum there from memory.
