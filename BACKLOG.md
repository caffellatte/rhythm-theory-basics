# Rhythm Theory Basics

A working backlog for a DAWless rhythm workbook. The author wants to keep improving at hearing, playing, and writing parts for drums, bass, and melody. Each cycle practices the same decision live, in stock TR-REC, and in Klangfeld Labs' DOOM OS on the SP-404MKII. The count and the musical exercise can travel to other machines.

## Where new producers get stuck

These questions guide lessons for producers working in a DAW piano roll or on a hardware sampler.

| What the learner experiences | What the lesson should let them try |
| --- | --- |
| “The kick feels wrong. Did I put it in the wrong place or let it ring too long?” | Keep the bass fixed. Move one kick, then return to the original and change only its tail. |
| “I changed three things and the groove improved. Which change did I hear?” | Save a reference take. Compare each edit separately against it at the same level. |
| “I released the pad or shortened the MIDI note. Why can I still hear the sample?” | Compare a tap and a hold on the dry sound, then listen to gated and one-shot playback. |
| “Someone says shortening the kick fixed their beat. Will it fix mine?” | Treat the producer's observation as a hypothesis. Try it on a copy of your own loop and describe what you actually hear. |
| “I copied the grid, but the caption puts the hit somewhere else.” | Speak the subdivisions, mark the attack, and listen against the pulse to find which description matches. |

## Pilot — kick placement and audible duration

The author's first listening question was how to tell a misplaced kick from a kick that rings too long. The exercise holds the bass fixed and changes only the second kick in each comparison. It opens a course that repeats with new material. [Listening page](pilot/kick-placement.html) · [Print card](pilot/kick-placement.pdf) · [Practice course](PRACTICE.md)

A 4×4 pad map names the sounds under the fingers. A left-to-right grid, spoken count, and short line of staff notation show their timing. The card should work beside the sampler in black and white.

## Following releases

Give each chapter and teaching section a short, sourced story that leads into something the reader can hear or play. Check the source alongside the musical example. Add a memory cue or a listening challenge where it helps readers recall a distinction and check their own answer.

- [ ] **Build the sol-fa foundation.** Start with pulse, tempo, meter, note values, and rests. Have readers speak subdivisions, clap accents, and write down a rhythm they hear. Add ties, syncopation, triplets, and swing through listening pairs. Sing *do–sol* and then a short melodic answer over the same drums.
- [ ] **Write four genre workshops.** Start with the same small kit. In hip-hop, compare the feel of two hi-hat timings against a backbeat. In house, place a hat between four steady kicks. In minimal techno, change one percussion hit over a repeating bass figure. In trance, shorten or lengthen an offbeat bass note beneath a repeating melody. Give each workshop an original example and a prompt to make a second version.
- [x] **Design the “Keep moving” mini-course.** The four-session cycle and progression through six musical problems are in the practice course.
- [ ] **Run the course with original takes.** Carry the routine through the genre and arrangement studies. Use the author's session log to select examples and revise the practice prompts.
